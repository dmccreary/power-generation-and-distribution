    Power Generation and Distribution¶

    Title: Power Generation and Distribution (modeled on Dunwoody College of Technology course ECDM2260)

    Target Audience: College undergraduate (two-year Associate of Applied Science students in electrical design, estimating and construction management, and adult learners in the electrical trades moving toward design roles)

    Prerequisites: Electrical Principles (AC/DC circuits, Ohm's law, power and energy basics), an introduction to the National Electrical Code (NEC), and Commercial & Industrial Principles (transformers, panels, feeders). College algebra and basic trigonometry are assumed.

    Course Overview¶

    This course gives students a foundational understanding of the entire power system, from the generation of electricity to the end users who consume it. Students follow electrical energy from the power plant, through step-up transformers, high-voltage transmission lines, substations and distribution feeders, to the service entrance of a commercial or industrial building. They study traditional generation (coal, natural gas, nuclear, hydroelectric) alongside renewable generation (wind, solar photovoltaic, geothermal, biomass) and energy storage.

    The course is taught with a specific focus on the needs of electrical designers. Designers create electrical systems that must be efficient, reliable, code-compliant and economical, and they can only do that if they understand the grid that feeds their designs: how voltage levels are chosen, how three-phase systems behave, why power factor and load profiles matter, how utilities bill and interconnect customers, and how faults and protective devices keep the system safe.

    The material matters now more than ever. Electrification of buildings and transportation, distributed solar and battery storage, data centers and microgrids are changing both the supply and demand sides of the grid. Graduates who understand the full path of power are better prepared to work as drafters, designers, estimators and project managers at engineering firms and electrical contractors.

    Main Topics Covered¶

    • Overview of the electric power system: generation, transmission, distribution and utilization
    • Fundamentals of AC power: three-phase systems, real/reactive/apparent power, power factor
    • Traditional generation: fossil fuel, nuclear and hydroelectric plants, generators and prime movers
    • Renewable generation: wind, solar photovoltaic, geothermal, biomass and other emerging sources
    • Energy storage: batteries, pumped hydro and other storage technologies
    • Transformers and substations: step-up/step-down, configurations, ratings and grounding
    • Transmission systems: high-voltage lines, losses, voltage regulation and grid interconnections
    • Distribution systems: primary and secondary feeders, service voltages, radial and network configurations
    • Load analysis: demand, load factor, diversity, load profiles and utility rate structures
    • Protection and reliability: faults, short-circuit basics, overcurrent protection, coordination and outage metrics
    • Service entrance and building-level distribution: metering, switchgear, panels, backup and standby power
    • Distributed energy resources, microgrids, grid interconnection and the codes and standards that govern them (NEC, NESC, IEEE)
    • Efficiency, sustainability, economics and environmental impacts of power choices

    Topics Not Covered¶

    • Detailed circuit theory and mathematical derivations (covered in Electrical Principles)
    • Detailed NEC article-by-article code study and branch-circuit/conductor sizing calculations (covered in NEC and design courses)
    • Power electronics and inverter circuit design
    • Utility-scale power system modeling, load-flow and transient stability software analysis
    • Detailed protective relay settings and arc-flash studies
    • Wholesale electricity market operations and energy trading
    • Hands-on lineworker skills and utility field operations
    • Detailed engineering design of generation plants or turbines

    Learning Outcomes¶

    After completing this course, students will be able to:

    Remember¶

    Retrieving, recognizing, and recalling relevant knowledge from long-term memory.

    • List the major stages of the power system: generation, transmission, distribution and utilization
    • Name the primary fuel and energy sources used to generate electricity and identify which are renewable
    • Recall typical voltage levels used for transmission, distribution and building service in North America
    • Define key terms such as real power, reactive power, power factor, demand, load factor, capacity factor and diversity
    • Identify the main components of a substation and a distribution feeder, such as transformers, breakers, switches and capacitor banks
    • State the purpose of key standards and organizations, including the NEC, NESC, IEEE and the local utility

    Understand¶

    Constructing meaning from instructional messages, including oral, written, and graphic communication.

    • Explain how a generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy
    • Describe why electricity is transmitted at high voltage and the role of transformers in reducing losses
    • Explain the differences between wye and delta three-phase connections and when each is used
    • Compare how steam, gas, hydro, wind and solar generation work and how each affects grid behavior
    • Summarize how power factor affects utility billing, equipment sizing and system efficiency
    • Interpret a single-line diagram showing the path from utility source to building loads
    • Explain how supply and demand are kept in balance on the grid

    Apply¶

    Carrying out or using a procedure in a given situation.

    • Calculate real, reactive and apparent power and power factor for single-phase and three-phase loads
    • Calculate line losses and voltage drop for a given feeder or transmission segment
    • Size a transformer for a given building load using demand factors
    • Estimate a facility's demand, load factor and monthly utility cost from a rate schedule
    • Estimate the annual energy output of a solar photovoltaic array or wind turbine from site data
    • Draw a basic single-line diagram for a building service, including utility connection, metering and main distribution
    • Select a power factor correction approach and size capacitors for a facility

    Analyze¶

    Breaking material into constituent parts and determining how the parts relate to one another and to an overall structure or purpose.

    • Analyze a load profile to identify peak demand, base load and opportunities for demand reduction
    • Differentiate radial, loop and network distribution configurations in terms of reliability and cost
    • Break down the causes and effects of faults and outages on a distribution system
    • Examine how adding distributed generation or storage changes power flow, protection and voltage on a feeder
    • Trace the energy losses from fuel source to end use and identify where efficiency can be improved
    • Compare the capacity factor, dispatchability and emissions of different generation technologies

    Evaluate¶

    Making judgments based on criteria and standards through checking and critiquing.

    • Evaluate whether a proposed electrical service design meets reliability, safety and code requirements
    • Critique a single-line diagram for protection coordination and single points of failure
    • Judge the trade-offs among generation options using cost, reliability, emissions and land-use criteria
    • Assess the economic payback of an energy efficiency, solar or storage investment for a facility
    • Evaluate the suitability of standby or backup power options for a critical facility
    • Defend a recommended utility service voltage and configuration using engineering and cost evidence

    Create¶

    Putting elements together to form a coherent or functional whole; reorganizing elements into a new pattern or structure.

    • Design a complete electrical service and distribution one-line for a commercial or industrial facility
    • Develop an energy plan that combines grid supply, on-site renewables and storage for a building
    • Produce a written and graphical report that traces a customer's power from generating station to meter
    • Formulate a reliability improvement proposal for an existing feeder or facility
    • Capstone project ideas:
      • Design a campus microgrid with solar, battery storage and backup generation, including single-line diagrams, load analysis and an economic summary
      • Prepare a "power path" study of a local building, tracing its supply back through the substation to the generating sources on the regional grid
      • Create an interactive or illustrated guide that teaches a new designer how a service request moves from utility interconnection to building panels
